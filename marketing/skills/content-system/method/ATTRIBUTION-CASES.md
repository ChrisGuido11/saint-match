# ATTRIBUTION-CASES.md — worked misattribution cases

**Layer: METHOD. Portable. Nothing here is brand-specific.**

Three verified cases, kept as teaching material. Each one is a real published
artifact from a real, competent publisher, and each fails in a **different**
way — which is the point of holding all three rather than one.

They exist because the abstract rule ("quotations need a source") is easy to
agree with and does not, on its own, catch any of these. Every one of them
survives an informal smell test. Two of them survive a *citation* check. Read the
mechanisms, not just the verdicts.

The specimens themselves live in the instance that collected them — for Saint
Match, `04-REFERENCE-ewtnmedia-st-joseph-calasanz.md`,
`05-REFERENCE-augustine-institute-st-augustine.md` and
`07-REFERENCE-ewtnmedia-st-bernard.md` in the Format B exemplar folder. The
lessons below do not depend on those files.

## The three mechanisms at a glance

| Case | Artifact | Verdict | Mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1** — Joseph Calasanz | Image line over the saint's name | **LOOSE-TO-WRONG** | **Formatting loss.** The source marked the quotation with *italics*; the transcription dropped them, and with them the citation frame |
| **2** — Bernard of Clairvaux | Caption clause, no quotation marks anywhere | **MISATTRIBUTED** | **Name collision.** Two men called Bernard, 400 years apart; the famous one absorbed the other's work |
| **3** — Augustine | Free-floating quotation | **UNVERIFIED, almost certainly misattributed** | **Aggregator laundering.** No source anywhere, circulating on aptness alone |

## Case 1 — St Joseph Calasanz: the citation frame that a typeface was carrying

**The line, as published on the image:**

> "Those who instruct many in justice will shine as stars for all eternity"
> — St. Joseph Calasanz

**What it actually is:** **Daniel 12:3**, near-verbatim Douay-Rheims — "they that
instruct many to justice, as stars for all eternity".

**And Calasanz did write it down.** It is in his *Memoriale al Card. M. A. Tonti*
(1621), where he introduces it with **"As Scripture says."** Both breviary
traditions carrying the Memoriale as the second reading for his memorial —
ICEL/US and UK/Ireland — **preserve an explicit quotation frame**.

So the two claims separate cleanly:

- **"Calasanz quoted Daniel 12:3."** Correct.
- **The line on an image under "— St. Joseph Calasanz."** Wrong.

**Verdict: LOOSE-TO-WRONG.** Nothing was invented. The words are real, the saint
really wrote them, the transcription is accurate. Only the frame is gone — and
the frame is what made it a quotation rather than an attribution.

### The mechanism — this is the reusable part

**The Liturgy of the Hours marks scriptural quotation with *italics*, not with
quotation marks.** In the breviary the Daniel line sits inside Calasanz's own
paragraph, in italic type, and **the italics are the only marker**. Copy that
paragraph into a caption box, a slide, a design tool or a plain-text note and the
italics disappear. What lands is a devout sentence under a saint's name, and
nothing anywhere in the copied text says otherwise.

**The proof that this is a formatting failure and not a judgement failure is in
the same reading: the next clause is Matthew 25:40** — and nobody attributes
*that* to Calasanz. Both clauses lost their italics. Only the one that is not
famous enough to be recognised on sight moved onto the saint's name. The person
transcribing was not being careless about sources; they were being defeated by a
lossy paste.

### The general lesson

> **A citation frame carried only by formatting does not survive transcription.**
> Any quotation harvested from a breviary, a lectionary, an Office of Readings
> excerpt, a devotional reprint, a PDF or a slide deck must be checked against
> the **formatted original**, not against the plain text that reached you.

Corollaries worth stating separately:

- **Recognisability is not a filter.** The famous quotation kept its frame by
  accident, because readers supply it. The obscure one did not. So the failure
  systematically targets the material a reader cannot check from memory — which
  is exactly the material that most needs the frame.
- **"It came from a liturgical book" is provenance, not verification.** Good
  provenance travelling through a lossy format produces a confident, traceable,
  wrong attribution.
- **A pipeline that quotes from formatted sources needs a transcription step
  that preserves emphasis**, or a rule that emphasis-bearing sources are
  re-opened at the original before anything is published from them.

### Also recorded: a wrong guess, marked as wrong

An earlier note in this material speculated that the line was **"the Piarist
motto"** and that motto-drift was the migration mechanism. **That was
unconfirmed and it is withdrawn.** The **Piarist motto is "Pietas et
Litterae"**. The real mechanism is the lost italics.

It is kept visible rather than deleted because it is its own small lesson: **a
plausible mechanism, offered to explain a real anomaly, is still a guess**, and
an unmarked guess in a research note is indistinguishable from a finding one
week later. Label speculation at the moment of writing it, or it will be
inherited as fact.

## Case 2 — St Bernard of Clairvaux: the misattribution with no quotation marks in it

**The clause, as published:**

> …known for his devotion to Mary and promotion of the **Memorare**.

**Verdict: MISATTRIBUTED.**

- **The Memorare is fifteenth century** — about **300 years after Bernard**
  (d. 1153). It first appears as part of the longer prayer *Ad sanctitatis tuae
  pedes, dulcissima Virgo Maria*, of which it is the opening section, later
  detached and prayed alone.
- **It was popularised by Fr Claude Bernard (1588–1641)**, a French priest who
  worked among prisoners and the condemned and printed and distributed
  **200,000+ leaflets** carrying it.
- **The name is the entire mechanism.** A prayer spread at scale by a *Bernard*
  migrates to the far more famous *Bernard*. No one decided this.
- **Source:** Herbert Thurston SJ, *Familiar Prayers: Their Origin and History*
  (1953).
- The modern form was **indulgenced by Pius IX, 11 December 1846** — an official
  act attaching to the prayer, not to any claim about Bernard's authorship.

### The general lesson

> **Attribution failures are not confined to quoted strings.** This sentence
> contains no quotation marks, quotes nothing, and puts no words in anyone's
> mouth. It is a **biographical claim about what someone is known for** — and a
> check that only inspects text inside quotation marks passes it without
> comment.

The claim has to be caught as an unsourced *fact* (does a source line support
"promoted the Memorare"?) rather than as an unsourced *quote*. In this
repository's gate that is **T1** and **T5**, not T3. Any adopting brand should
confirm its own gate has a check that reads assertions of this shape, because it
is the cheapest place for a misattribution to hide.

Second lesson, on the class of error:

> **Name collision is a standing generator of misattribution, and it is
> asymmetric.** The attribution always drifts toward the more famous holder of
> the name, never away. So whenever a claim attaches a work to a famous person
> who shares a name with a less famous contemporary or successor — and
> especially when the dates are loose — check the dates first. A 300-year gap is
> not subtle once anyone looks.

### The same post's other half — kept together on purpose

The **image quote on that same post is genuine**:

> "In dangers, in doubts, in difficulties, think of Mary, call upon Mary…"

— *Homiliae super Missus est*, **Hom. II.17, PL 183:70–71**, quoted by **Pius
XII, *Doctor Mellifluus* (1953) §31**, which is the citation to prefer in
practice: an official act, stable, freely available, checkable.

**One artifact, one misattribution and one properly sourced quotation, side by
side.** This is the argument for grading **claims** rather than **sources**. A
publisher is not "reliable" or "unreliable"; the unit that carries a grade is the
individual line, which is precisely what the dossier convention is for.

## Case 3 — St Augustine: the quote with no origin, and the honest limit

**The line:**

> "Lord, it's hard to follow you, but it's impossible to leave you"

**Verdict: UNVERIFIED, and almost certainly MISATTRIBUTED.**

**What was searched, and what came back:**

- **Zero hits** across the augustinus.it Latin/Italian *Opera Omnia*.
- A **full offline grep of the Latin *Confessiones*** (523,997 characters) finds
  **`impossibile` zero times in the entire work**. Not in this phrase — *at all*.
- **All** circulation is quote-aggregators and social posts. **No copy anywhere
  carries a work-or-section citation.** The earliest instance found is **2016**,
  in Portuguese.

**The honest limit, stated because it changes the verdict's strength:** the
definitive **Corpus Augustinianum Gissense** is paywalled and **was not
searched**. That is why this reads **"almost certainly"** and not
**"definitively"**. The corpus that was searched is large and the phrase's
absence from it is strong evidence; it is not proof, and the write-up says so.

### The general lesson

> **State the boundary of the search, not just its result.** "Not found" is a
> claim about the searcher as much as about the text. A finding that names the
> corpus searched, the corpus skipped and the reason is auditable and can be
> upgraded later by whoever gets access. A bare "misattributed" is neither, and
> it hardens into false certainty the moment someone quotes it.

Supporting lesson, on what the evidence pattern itself means:

> **Absence of a citation across an entire circulation is itself the finding.**
> A genuine line from a major author, in wide circulation, accumulates
> work-and-section references — someone always adds them. A line that has
> circulated for a decade and picked up **none**, in any language, is
> circulating on **aptness**. Aptness is why it spreads and is not evidence that
> it is real.

Note the shape of this one against Case 1: there, the words were real and the
frame was lost. Here there are no words to lose — the line is fluent, moving,
perfectly Augustinian in sentiment, and appears to come from nowhere. **That
combination is the signature of invention**, and it is the failure mode a
language model reproduces most fluently.

### The genuine replacement

There is no need to reach for the spurious line, because the authentic locus is
better and it covers the same ground:

> ***Tractates on the Gospel of John*, Tractate 27.9** —
> *"Si a te recedimus, ad quem ibimus?"* / **"If we abandon You, to whom shall we
> go?"**

And the pleasing part: **§2 of the same tractate** treats *"This is a hard
saying; who can hear it?"* — so **both halves of the spurious quotation appear,
authentically, in one locus**. The hard following and the impossible leaving are
both there, in Augustine, with a citation.

> **The general move: when a spurious quote is popular, find what it was
> reaching for.** It is nearly always reaching for something the author really
> did write. Replacing it is a better outcome than banning it, because a ban
> leaves the writer with a hole and the hole is what invited the invention.

## What an adopting brand should take from these three

1. **Grade claims, not sources.** Case 2's post carries a misattribution and a
   perfect citation in the same frame.
2. **Check the shape of the transmission, not only the content.** Case 1 was
   destroyed by a typeface.
3. **Cover unquoted claims.** Case 2 has no quotation marks in it.
4. **Publish the limits of a negative result.** Case 3's "almost certainly" is
   load-bearing and was nearly written as "definitively".
5. **Carry a replacement, not just a refusal.** Case 3's Tractate 27.9.
6. **Mark speculation as speculation the moment it is written.** The withdrawn
   "Piarist motto" note in Case 1.
